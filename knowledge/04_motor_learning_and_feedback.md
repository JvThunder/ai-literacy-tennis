# Motor Learning and Feedback Principles
Source: summary of coaching research (sample document)

## External focus of attention
Instructions that direct attention to the effect on the ball or target, such as clear the net by a metre, tend to improve performance and learning more than instructions about body parts. For beginners, cue the outcome of the shot rather than joint angles, for example let the racket sweep the ball toward the far corner.

## Limit feedback to one or two points
Beginners learn more when they receive a small number of focused corrections. Presenting every deviation at once overloads attention. Prioritise by size of deviation, how often it appears and how much it affects the shot.

## Use video as evidence
Showing a learner frames from their own swing links the verbal feedback to what they did. Video feedback works best when it is paired with a clear instruction for the next practice, and when the learner can compare the key moments of preparation, contact and finish.

## Repeat, then progress
A drill should be repeated until the learner succeeds on about seven of ten attempts. If it feels too hard, simplify by removing the ball or slowing the feed. When it feels easy, add movement, a faster feed or a target.

## Practice should resemble play
Fed balls help learn a movement, but skills transfer better when practice includes decisions and variation. Move from stationary balls to alternating sides and short rallies as soon as the movement is reliable.

## Be honest about uncertainty
Feedback should say when the footage is unclear. A short note that the wrist was hidden, or that the camera angle limits what can be judged, is more useful than a confident but wrong correction.
